💧 JalUrja

Water Productivity as a Service · Challenge 01: Sustainable Agriculture (Energy, Water & Productivity)

Shared solar irrigation for smallholders, delivered as a metered service, with government support paid for verified water productivity rather than for pumping.

1. The problem we solve

Agriculture uses nearly 90% of India's freshwater withdrawals and employs over 40% of its workforce. Smallholders irrigate by habit or fixed schedule, often with diesel or heavily subsidised pumps. Two things go wrong: diesel makes water expensive, while solar or free power makes it nearly free, which invites over-pumping. Solar alone swaps an energy problem for a groundwater problem. JalUrja separates clean energy from unlimited water.

2. How it works

☀️ Shared solar hub

An FPO, cooperative or local entrepreneur owns the solar pump, tank, drip network and smart meters. The farmer buys no equipment.

🛰️ Sense and decide

Satellite (Sentinel-2), weather forecast, soil-moisture nodes and crop stage feed a FAO-56 soil-water balance. It sets when and how much to irrigate, and skips irrigation when rain is forecast.

📱 Voice-first advice

Marathi/Hindi voice and SMS: Irrigate · Wait · Rain expected. Four large icons only: Water, Weather, Crop, Payment.

💰 Pay per metered litre

UPI pay-as-you-go for water actually delivered. A proposed Water Productivity Credit (WPC) reduces the bill only for farms that stay within their seasonal water budget and achieve a target kg of crop per m³.

Inspiration: the EU WATER-BEE demonstration reported 21% average and up to 44% irrigation-water savings from sensor-based scheduling across 12 sites (CORDIS). JalUrja adapts this to Indian smallholder conditions by adding shared ownership, satellite-based plot intelligence that needs no sensor on every field, and an incentive linked to water productivity.

Payback depends on the capital subsidy: without it, it is well over five years. WPC mainly works as a water-budget discipline tool: against diesel the farmer already saves money, so WPC matters most where power is free or heavily subsidised. The 40% rate is a proposed pilot policy, not an existing government rate.
